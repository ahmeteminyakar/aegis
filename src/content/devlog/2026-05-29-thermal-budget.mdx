The first entry said there was a thermal budget on a napkin and a
control-loop sketch that had not been simulated. This post takes the
napkin and runs it. There is still no bench, no module picked, no
firmware. Just the physics and the arithmetic, and what they rule out
before a single part is ordered.

The question underneath the whole device is simple to state. Cool a
pulse point on the inner wrist, and does the wearer feel cooler. If the
answer is no, there is nothing to build. So that is where the napkin
starts.

## The hypothesis

The inner wrist puts the radial artery close to the surface. A
thermoelectric cooler (a TEC, a Peltier element) pressed against that
skin can pull heat out of a small patch. The bet is that a few degrees
of local cooling there reads to the brain as cooler, out of proportion
to the actual heat removed. The reference device, Embr Wave, runs on
exactly this bet. The mechanism is not controversial. What is worth
checking is whether the felt effect is real, and on what terms.

Embr's public page is instructive for what it does not disclose. No
contact temperature drop, no battery capacity in mAh, only perceptual
claims. That is the gap this notebook is trying to fill: the
thermodynamics behind the perception.

## What the comfort science actually says

Yes, with two conditions the marketing tends to skip.

First, the stimulus has to be dynamic. Static local cooling or warming
of a small skin area has an insignificant effect on whole-body thermal
sensation. The effect shows up only when the stimulus changes over time
([Wang, Zhang, Arens, Huizenga 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2006.06.035)).
A steady cold plate on the wrist does close to nothing for overall
comfort. A wave does something. Later work pins the pleasure
specifically to the rate of change of skin temperature, not the
endpoint it settles at ([Parkinson and de Dear 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/ina.12859)).

Second, the effect is thermal alliesthesia: a transient, pleasurable
shift in perceived comfort that decays as the body returns to
thermoneutral. It is a perception, not a change in core temperature
([Parkinson, de Dear, Candido 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/09613218.2015.1082334)).
The device is not cooling your core in any thermodynamic sense. It
produces a sensation. That is the honest framing, and it is enough,
because comfort is a perception to begin with. For reference, the
neutral skin-temperature band at the wrist sits around 34.3 to 34.6 C,
inside a whole-skin neutral zone of 30 to 36 C
([Yu et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2023.110567)).
The device is nudging a signal that lives in a narrow band.

How large is the felt effect. The figure Embr markets is feeling up to
9 F cooler. That number is the best-case subgroup, women in a warm
room, from a company-affiliated study, and it is a perceived metric,
not an air-temperature equivalent and not a core-temperature change.
The reported average corrective power for dynamic waves of roughly 2 to
3 C local dT is 4.5 to 5.9 F, about 2.5 to 3.3 C, within three minutes
([Wang Z. et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2019.106443)).
The average is the number to design against. The 9 F is a headline.

## What the thermodynamics says

Now the hardware side, where most of the constraints live.

A TEC moving heat out of the skin does not make that heat disappear. It
moves it to the hot side and adds the electrical work it took to move
it. The identity is Qh = Qc + Pin: the hot side must shed the cooling
load plus the full electrical input. On a wristband there is no fan and
no real heatsink, just a small thermal mass and the back of the device
against air. That mass buffers the hot side for a while, then saturates.
In the wearable-scale experiment this budget leans on, the skin-contact
temperature drop reached about 2.5 C and held for roughly ten minutes
before the buffer saturated and the cooling collapsed, near 605 seconds
([Materials 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/ma18071576), with a companion
simulation in [Materials 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/ma17133266)).
Steady-state cooling on a wristband is not on the table. The hot side
wins eventually.

Then the battery, which is the harder wall. A realistic wristband cell
is about 350 mAh, 1.30 Wh at 3.7 V (PKCELL LP552530 class), bracketed by
0.93 Wh at the small end and 1.85 Wh at the large. At 1 W continuous
that is about 78 minutes per charge, which alone pushes toward
duty-cycling. But the sharper limit is the discharge rate. A small pouch
cell of this size tolerates roughly 1.5C continuous. At 1.30 Wh that is
a ceiling near 1.95 W. A 1 W draw sits comfortably under it. A 2 W draw
would demand 0.54 A, which is 1.54C, over the limit. The cell cannot
source 2 W. It is rate-limited before it is capacity-limited. That is
the single most useful thing the napkin says: the upper bound on
instantaneous cooling power is set by the cell's C-rate, not by the TEC
or the COP.

For context on how small this power budget is, an independent
precedent: a flexible mesh-armband TEC patch from UCSD ran at 0.2 W or
less per patch ([Hong et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aaw0536)).
Wrist-scale thermal devices live in the sub-watt to low-watt range.

One number for proportion. The electronics, the MCU plus BLE plus the
temperature and motion sensors, draw about 23 mW active, roughly 2
percent of the 1 W TEC peak. The TEC dominates the energy budget so
completely that the control logic is almost free by comparison.
Optimize the TEC duty cycle, not the firmware's milliamps.

## The budget, run

The sim is pure-stdlib arithmetic over the constants above. Output
verbatim:

```
peak draw (electrical):      1.00 W
avg draw at 35% duty:        0.35 W
cooling delivered (Qc):      0.70 W
hot-side dissipation (Qh):   1.70 W  (= Qc + Pin, heatsink target)
continuous-on runtime:       78.0 min
PWM-duty runtime:           222.9 min
bursts per charge (90s):       52  (energy-only; ignores PCM saturation, rest, converter loss)
discharge ceiling (1.5C):    1.95 W
peak draw within ceiling:     yes (1.00 W vs 1.95 W)
note: 2.00 W draw = 0.54 A = 1.54C, exceeds 1.5C max; not feasible on this cell
```

Two lines deserve a flag. The hot-side figure uses the cooling COP
correctly: at COP 0.7 and 1 W in, the cold side moves 0.7 W and the hot
side has to shed 1.7 W. A first draft of this sim had the formula wrong,
treating the 1 W as the heat pumped rather than the electrical input,
which inflated the heatsink target to 2.43 W. The corrected identity is
Qh = Qc + Pin. Separately, datasheets quote hot-side loads up to about
2.6 times a module's rated Qmax near its current limit. That is a
different reference point, not this 1.7 W operating case. And the 52
bursts per charge line is an energy-only idealization. It divides
battery energy by per-burst energy and ignores PCM saturation, the rest
windows between bursts, and converter losses. Treat it as an upper
bound, not a spec.

## The synthesis

Here is where the two halves meet, and it is the part that makes the
device coherent rather than a compromise.

The perception science says the effect only works if the stimulus is
dynamic: waves, not a steady plate. The hardware says steady-state is
impossible anyway, because the hot side saturates in about ten minutes
and the battery gives about 78 minutes at 1 W continuous. Both sides
point at the same answer. Cool in bursts, rest, repeat. A 90-second
burst at 1 W followed by a rest window keeps the average draw near
0.35 W, stretches runtime past three and a half hours, lets the hot side
recover between bursts, and, critically, is the exact dynamic profile
the comfort research says you need.

Burst-cycling is not a battery-saving hack bolted onto a cooling device.
It is what produces the felt effect and what the hardware can sustain,
at the same time. The adaptive PWM duty loop is the product, not a
concession to the battery.

## The figures, and where they come from

Every number above, with its evidence class. Peer-reviewed figures are
stated plainly. Manufacturer guides, datasheets, and arithmetic
estimates are marked as such. The one marketing claim is marked
marketing.

```
quantity                    value             evidence        source
--------------------------  ----------------  --------------  ---------------------
TEC peak electrical draw    ~1 W              peer-reviewed   Materials 2025
cooling COP (single stage)  0.3 to 0.7        mfr guides      Meerstetter, Sheetak
skin-contact dT, poor sink  ~2.5 C for ~10 m  peer-reviewed   Materials 2025
hot-side buffer saturation  ~605 s            peer-reviewed   Materials 2025 + 2024
realistic wrist cell        350 mAh, 1.30 Wh  datasheet       PKCELL LP552530
1.5C discharge ceiling      ~1.95 W           datasheet+calc  PKCELL + arithmetic
runtime, 1 W continuous     ~78 min           estimate        1.30 Wh / 1.0 W
runtime, 35% PWM duty       ~223 min          estimate        1.30 Wh / 0.35 W
2 W continuous draw         not feasible      datasheet       1.54C exceeds 1.5C
electronics active draw     ~23 mW (~2%)      datasheet       STM32L4, nRF52840
wrist neutral skin temp     34.3 to 34.6 C    peer-reviewed   Yu 2023
dynamic dT felt effect      4.5 to 5.9 F avg  peer-reviewed   Wang 2020
Embr "up to 9 F cooler"     best-case subset  marketing       embrlabs.com
```

A caveat on the strongest-looking rows. The skin-dT and saturation
figures rest substantially on one experimental paper and its companion
simulation. On-point and credible, but not a broad multi-study consensus
on the exact numbers. The COP range comes from manufacturer design
guides, not raw datasheet curve reads. The runtime figures are
arithmetic from cell energy, shown in the source column so the math is
checkable.

## What this means, and what is still unknown

What the napkin settles. The device is a sub-watt, burst-cycled,
perception-targeting wrist cooler. Peak draw is capped near 1 W by the
cell's C-rate, not by ambition. The hot side, not the cold side, is the
hard thermal problem. The control loop's job is to schedule dynamic
bursts inside a roughly ten-minute hot-side budget and a 1.95 W power
ceiling.

What it does not settle, and what the bench has to answer:

- The real skin-contact dT this enclosure can hold, and for how long,
  with whatever thermal mass actually fits. The 2.5 C and 605 s figures
  come substantially from one paper and its companion sim.
- The actual TEC module. The 24 by 44 mm wearable-scale part in the
  literature is a precedent, not a part number. COP here is from design
  guides, not curves off a chosen module.
- The control loop itself, still a sketch. Burst length, rest length,
  and how skin-temp, ambient, and IMU inputs schedule the next burst.
